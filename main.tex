\begin{abstract}
Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is rarely aimless. Analysts usually have a goal in mind, even when they do not know exactly what it is. Yet the systems that assist EDA mostly predict an analyst's \emph{next step}: the next query, action, or chart. Alternatively, they ask the analyst to label what they want. We argue that database systems should instead anticipate where an investigation is heading: its \emph{destination}, the pattern, exception, or set of entities the exploration is converging on. Anticipating destinations requires four shifts in how systems support EDA. Interest should be inferred implicitly from exploration rather than elicited through feedback. Inference should be conditioned on the whole trajectory rather than the current step. Systems should recognise that analytical interest takes several forms. Finally, they should be evaluated with reproducible benchmarks that plant destinations of known kinds and measure how early each is recovered. We describe such a benchmark and use it to examine Multi-Dimensional Interestingness (MDI), a scorer that is agnostic to the query language, alongside simple history-based baselines. On controlled trajectories, history-conditioned scorers recognise a destination's context as soon as a query reveals it. They recover structural and distributional patterns within about a step of the evidence that distinguishes them. The benchmark also exposes what remains hard: compositional patterns, destinations that share a context with a competitor, and detours. We close with a research agenda on interfaces that act on predicted destinations and on studies that test whether doing so accelerates EDA.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=============================================================================

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is an iterative process of examining data, formulating questions, and revising an investigation in response to what is found. It includes profiling data quality as well as discovering patterns, and analysts' goals and working context shape which activities are useful~\cite{wongsuphasawat2019edagoals}. Those goals are often loosely formed. An analyst may be testing a hunch, or looking for a trend or anomaly they will only recognise when they see it. Each query result gives the analyst candidate data for the next step. It does not tell a system which of those data matter, or which will matter as the investigation develops. A rare value may be a distraction, while an ordinary-looking row may be central to a developing explanation.

Systems that assist EDA have taken two broad approaches. Explore-by-example and active-learning systems ask analysts to label tuples or regions until the system learns what they want~\cite{dimitriadouAIDEActiveLearningBased2016,qinRankingDesiredTuples2021}. Recommendation systems instead predict the next thing to show: the next query~\cite{eirinakiQuerieCollaborativeDatabase2013}, the next action~\cite{dezoysa2025exploract}, or the most deviating chart for the current query~\cite{vartakSeedbEfficientDatadriven2015}. The first interrupts the analysis it is meant to support. The second answers a narrower question than the analyst is asking. Analysts rarely issue a query for its own sake. Even when they cannot yet say what they are looking for, each query moves them towards something, and the direction becomes clearer as the session unfolds.

\textbf{A running example.}
Consider two analysts exploring a shipments table (Figure~\ref{fig:example}). Analyst A looks at shipments from Asia, narrows to technology products, and then looks at urgent ones. Analyst B also starts in Asia, narrows to healthcare products, and then looks at expensive ones. Both then open the same broad result. A system that predicts the next step, or explains the current one~\cite{deutchFEDEXExplainabilityFramework2022}, sees the same result and gives both analysts the same answer. This is the litmus test for a destination-aware system: it must tell the two apart. What each needs from that result is different. A is converging on the relationship between technology and urgency in Asia, B on unusually priced healthcare shipments. We call these \emph{destinations}. A system that anticipates them could surface A's urgent technology shipments and B's high-priced healthcare shipments before either analyst has finished getting there.

We argue that \textbf{database systems should support exploratory analysis by anticipating where an analyst's investigation is heading, inferred implicitly from how their exploration unfolds, rather than by predicting their next step or asking them for feedback}. Predicting the destination comes first. Designing interfaces and interaction paradigms that shorten the path to it, and so accelerate EDA as a whole, comes after. We identify four principles such systems should follow:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, topsep=2pt, itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Implicit.} Infer interest from exploration rather than interrupting it with requests for labels or examples.
  \item \textbf{Trajectory-conditioned.} Condition on the history of the session, which reveals direction, persistence, and change.
  \item \textbf{Plural interest.} Recognise that destinations take different forms (structural, distributional, compositional) that no single interestingness measure captures.
  \item \textbf{Reproducible destination benchmarks.} Evaluate by planting destinations of known kinds and measuring how early each is recovered.
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are the destination framing of EDA assistance (Section~\ref{sec:destinations}), the four principles as design requirements (Section~\ref{sec:principles}), a positioning of existing work against them (Section~\ref{sec:related}), and a benchmark design with a first instantiation that shows both promise and failure modes (Section~\ref{sec:first-step}). Section~\ref{sec:agenda} sets out a research agenda, including how acting on destinations could accelerate EDA and how that should be validated with analysts.

\section{From Next Steps to Destinations}
\label{sec:destinations}
%=============================================================================

An exploration session is a sequence of queries $Q_1,\ldots,Q_n$ over a dataset $T$ with results $R_1,\ldots,R_n$. We write
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}_q=((Q_1,R_1),\ldots,(Q_q,R_q))
\end{equation}
for the trajectory observed after $q$ steps.

\textbf{Destinations.}
A destination $d$ is what the trajectory is converging on. It need not be fixed in advance, or known precisely to the analyst. It can be loosely formed at the start and take shape as the exploration proceeds, which is why a system must infer it from the trajectory rather than ask for it. A destination has two faces: a \emph{description} $\varphi_d$, the pattern or reason the data matter, and an \emph{extension} $E_d\subseteq T$, the tuples that instantiate it. There are different kinds of destinations. \emph{Patterns} are structural, distributional, or compositional regularities, such as an association between technology and urgency in Asia. \emph{Exceptions} are tuples that break an expected pattern. \emph{Entities} are specific objects that matter in their own right, such as a newly discovered object in an astronomical survey. Every pattern has an extension, but an extension does not identify its pattern: the same rows can instantiate several descriptions. Exceptions bridge the two, since they have both a description (the pattern they violate) and a small extension.

\textbf{Analytical interest and scope.}
We use \emph{analytical interest} to mean the potential value of data to the analyst's evolving investigation. It is latent and contextual. It differs from \emph{statistical interestingness}, which values data by a fixed property such as deviation or association strength, and from \emph{exposure}, since presence in a result does not mean a row was inspected. We consider a single analyst exploring one tabular dataset through selections, drill-downs, comparisons, and revisits, in sessions of tens of steps. We assume results carry stable entity identities. We do not assume access to other users' sessions. EDA sessions are typically sparse, and although a corpus can supply priors when one exists, a system should not depend on it. We also require scoring to be \emph{agnostic to the query language}, using only results, so that it can sit beneath SQL, dataframe, visual, or natural-language front-ends. Aggregation, joins that change entity granularity, and incomplete logs are outside this initial scope.

\textbf{The task.}
After observing $\mathcal{H}_q$, a system receives a result $R$, typically a broader result the analyst opens or returns to, and ranks its tuples so that $E_d\cap R$ comes first:
\begin{equation}
  s_q(t)=f(t,R,\mathcal{H}_q),\qquad t\in R.
  \label{eq:task}
\end{equation}
In this paper we evaluate this tuple-level form. The candidates are the rows of $R$, which may include rows the analyst has never seen. The system re-ranks a result the analyst has opened rather than searching the collection. Proposing destinations from the whole of $T$, as ranked descriptions together with their extensions and including rows no query has yet returned, is the longer-term goal (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}). A prediction is valuable to the extent that it is right \emph{early}: the fewer steps a system needs to identify a destination, the more of the path an interface could shorten. Another way to think about it is instead of predicting what the analyst will do next, we try to predict what they will find interesting multiple steps in the future.

\textbf{Anticipation over time.}
A destination is anticipated, rather than merely recognised, if the system identifies it before the trajectory arrives. We therefore evaluate the scorer after every prefix of a trajectory $Q_1,\ldots,Q_n$ heading to $d$, against the same result $R$:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_q=\mathrm{prec@}k\big(s_q,\,E_d\cap R\big),\qquad q=0,\ldots,n,
  \label{eq:prefix}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{prec@}k$ is the fraction of the $k$ highest-scored tuples of $R$ that belong to $E_d$. The \emph{earliness} of the scorer is the first step from which it stays right,
\begin{equation}
  q^{*}=\min\{\,q : \pi_{q'}\ge\tau\ \text{for all}\ q'\ge q\,\},
  \label{eq:earliness}
\end{equation}
for a threshold $\tau$ above random, with $q^{*}=n+1$ if no such step exists. The analyst could then have been spared $n-q^{*}$ steps. Earliness rewards a scorer that commits once the evidence allows and keeps to that commitment. It penalises one that is right by accident and then changes its mind. Earliness is close in spirit to the distinctiveness studied in goal recognition design, which bounds how long an agent can act before its goal is revealed~\cite{kerenGoalRecognitionDesign2014}. Unlike goal recognition, however, the candidate destinations are not enumerated in advance.

\textbf{Why not predict the next result?}
Intuitively, the more often a piece of data is accessed during a session, the more likely it is to interest the analyst. It is therefore natural to treat tuples that reappear in the next result as relevant. This confuses exposure with interest. When analyst A narrows ``shipments from Asia'' to ``technology shipments from Asia'', every Asian technology shipment reappears by set containment, whether or not A cares about it. A's destination is narrower: the urgent technology shipments that reveal the relationship A is investigating. Analyst B's first step returns the same Asian shipments, so recurrence alone cannot tell the two analysts apart. Reappearance reflects the structure of the queries, not where the analyst is heading. Defining the target as the destination's extension separates the two, and a model that merely tracks recurring rows gains nothing. We test this directly in Section~\ref{sec:first-step}.

\section{Four Principles for Destination-Aware EDA}
\label{sec:principles}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Implicit}
Interest should be inferred from the exploration itself, not elicited alongside it. Explicit feedback takes many forms: ranking tuples~\cite{dimitriadouAIDEActiveLearningBased2016}, validating examples~\cite{rezig2021dice}, answering membership queries~\cite{bonifati2014interactive}, or judging similarity~\cite{mottin2014exemplar}. Each asks the analyst to step out of exploration and into evaluation. Exploratory analysis already carries substantial intrinsic load, because the analyst holds hypotheses, intermediate findings, and possible next steps in working memory~\cite{sweller1988cognitive}. Feedback requests add extraneous load on top of it~\cite{sweller1998cognitive}, and switching between the two modes has measurable costs of its own~\cite{monsell2003task,rubinstein2001executive}. These costs accumulate as exploration deepens, discouraging feedback precisely when it would be most informative. Asking also assumes the analyst can say what they want: someone looking for a trend they have not yet seen cannot label examples of it. Rather than asking analysts to \emph{describe} what they find interesting, a system can \emph{observe} what they pursue~\cite{kelly2003implicit}. When an analyst reformulates a query or drills into a subset, the choice itself is evidence. A system must therefore work from what any front-end produces, and accept that this evidence is ambiguous. Explicit feedback remains valuable when behaviour cannot resolve that ambiguity, but as a fallback rather than the default.

\subsection{Trajectory-conditioned}
The unit of evidence should be the trajectory, not the current step. Information-foraging theory describes analysts following an information scent~\cite{pirolli2005information}, and their successive choices form a trail that points somewhere. A single step is ambiguous: the same query can serve several goals, and the same goal can be reached through different queries. Over several steps, some evidence persists (A keeps returning to technology in Asia) while other evidence is incidental, such as a filter applied once and abandoned. Interests also change as analysts abandon threads, take detours, or switch goals entirely. A system must therefore accumulate evidence across steps, let stale evidence fade, and distinguish a persistent direction from incidental narrowing and a genuine switch from a detour. Section~\ref{sec:first-step} shows that the obvious signal (``whatever was narrowed is where they are going'') is not enough.

\subsection{Plural Analytical Interest}
Destinations take different forms, and a system must be able to recognise each. No single interestingness measure consistently predicts what analysts find interesting across domains and tasks~\cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019}, and the EDA literature distinguishes several qualities of interesting results~\cite{chansonInterestingnessMeasuresExploratory2025a}. Drawing on the treatment of interestingness measures by Hilderman and Hamilton~\cite{robertj.hildermanKnowledgeDiscoveryMeasures2001}, we distinguish three kinds of pattern destination (Table~\ref{tab:kinds}). \emph{Structural} destinations are relationships between attributes, evidenced by association measures~\cite{goodman1991rule}. \emph{Distributional} destinations are concentrations, skews, and tails, evidenced by diversity measures~\cite{shannon1948mathematical,gini1921measurement,bergerDiversityPlanktonicForaminifera1970,mcintoshIndexDiversityRelation1967}. \emph{Compositional} destinations are contrasts that hold only within particular sub-populations. Each kind also has a confound that a system must not mistake for interest. A system must therefore combine evidence of several kinds on a common scale, and infer from the session which kind matters rather than fixing it in advance.

\subsection{Reproducible Destination Benchmarks}
Progress requires benchmarks that test whether a system recovers destinations of known kinds, and how early it does so. Human studies establish usefulness, but they are expensive, and an open dataset alone does not make them reusable: reproduction also needs the tasks, histories, and relevance judgements. Recorded sessions support comparison~\cite{dezoysa2025exploract}, but agreement with an observed next action does not show that a model recovered the analyst's goal. Other fields complement realistic data with diagnostic benchmarks that isolate specific capabilities~\cite{johnson2017clevr,ribeiro2020beyond}. A destination benchmark should therefore plant destinations of each kind in a dataset and generate trajectories towards them. It should control how ambiguous the early steps are and whether trajectories detour or switch, and score how early each destination is recovered. Paired trajectories that reach the same result while heading to different destinations turn the litmus test of Figure~\ref{fig:example} into a measurement. Goal-driven workload generators provide the realistic counterpart. SIMBA models analysis goals as goal queries reached through valid interaction sequences~\cite{purichAdaptiveBenchmarkModeling2025}. Its goals are destinations judged by extension, once its aggregate dashboard queries are mapped back to the rows they summarise.

\textbf{Implicit or explicit.}
AIDE uses active learning to acquire relevance labels on selected samples~\cite{dimitriadouAIDEActiveLearningBased2016}, and DEXPLORER learns desired tuples and their ranking from labels and pairwise preferences~\cite{qinRankingDesiredTuples2021}. Both learn a target region, which is a destination judged by extension, but only by asking. Many systems already recommend without feedback. YMALDB uses frequent attribute sets to suggest related tuples~\cite{drosouYmaldbExploringRelational2013}. SeeDB ranks visualisations by deviation from a reference~\cite{vartakSeedbEfficientDatadriven2015}, and Voyager combines statistical and perceptual criteria~\cite{wongsuphasawat2016voyager}. Removing feedback is therefore not new. What these systems lack is a model of where the analyst is going.

\textbf{Per-step or trajectory.}
FEDEX explains why a single step is interesting. By default it applies exceptionality to filters, joins, and unions and diversity to group-bys, and it also accepts user-defined measures. It then identifies the sets of rows that contribute most to the step's interestingness~\cite{deutchFEDEXExplainabilityFramework2022}. It is implicit and supports several measures, but its output depends only on the last step's input and output. The two analysts in Figure~\ref{fig:example} receive the same explanation. Per-step interestingness is best seen as \emph{evidence} that a destination-aware system accumulates over a trajectory. Milo et al.\ predict which interestingness measure suits a step from session context~\cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019}, and Chanson et al.\ infer user beliefs from past interactions and other users' activity~\cite{chanson2019belief}. Both condition on history, but they score the current step rather than anticipating a destination.

\textbf{Next step or destination.}
QueRIE recommends queries using collaborative evidence from tuple or query-fragment profiles~\cite{eirinakiQuerieCollaborativeDatabase2013}, and ExplorAct predicts the next action type and column from exploration histories~\cite{dezoysa2025exploract}. Query Steering proposed navigation guided by user interests and interaction histories~\cite{ugurcetintemelQuerySteeringInteractive2013}. ATENA generates whole exploration sessions with reinforcement learning~\cite{barelAutomaticallyGeneratingData2020}, which is a different problem from forecasting a particular analyst's destination from a prefix. A next action may be an intermediate comparison, a data-quality check, or an attempt to reject a hypothesis. Predicting it accurately does not show that a model knows where the analyst is heading, although its representations could inform a model that does.

\textbf{Goal recognition and interaction-based inference.}
Inferring a goal from an observed trajectory has been analysed extensively by the classical planning community. Plan recognition as planning ranks a given set of candidate goals by how well the cost of reaching each explains the observations~\cite{ramirezPlanRecognitionPlanning2009,ramirezProbabilisticPlanRecognition2010,mastersCostbasedGoalRecognition2019}. These methods assume what EDA lacks: a model of the available actions, a planner to cost them, and a known set of candidate goals. Queries are not actions with modelled effects, and destinations are descriptions over the data that are not enumerated in advance and may only take shape during the session. Generating candidate destinations is itself a data-management problem (Section~\ref{sec:agenda}). Visual analytics comes closer, inferring interest from interactions without a planner. Interaction patterns have been used to infer the analytic task~\cite{gotz2009behavior} and properties of the user~\cite{brown2014waldo}, and analytic provenance has characterised how exploration unfolds~\cite{battleCharacterizingExploratoryVisual2019}. Follow the Clicks anticipates which data points an analyst will select next~\cite{ottley2019clicks}, and Competing Models maintains a posterior over which attributes drive exploration~\cite{monadjemi2021competing}. Both model a single kind of interest, proximity in some attribute space, and target the next interaction. We adapt Competing Models as a baseline in our preliminary experiments.

\textbf{LLM-based exploration.}
LLM-based assistants have been shown to generate analyses and visualisations from natural-language goals~\cite{dibia2023lida,ma2023insightpilot}. They make stating a goal cheaper, but they start from what the analyst says. Early in exploration, the goal may not yet be well formed, even to the analyst, so there is no guarantee that the assistant finds what the analyst is heading towards. Destination inference complements them. A trajectory-conditioned estimate can ground such an assistant, and an LLM could in turn propose or verbalise destination descriptions $\varphi_d$, to be judged on the same benchmark.

\textbf{Implicit recommendation.}
The recommender-systems literature shows that useful signals can be extracted from ordinary behaviour through confidence-weighted factorisation~\cite{hu2008collaborative}, pairwise ranking~\cite{rendle2009bpr}, temporal dynamics~\cite{koren2009collaborative}, and session-based models~\cite{hidasi2016session}. It also separates exposure from preference~\cite{liang2016exposure,lin2024recrec} and models changing intent~\cite{wang2018modeling}. Semantic Interaction shows that analytical interactions can steer the underlying models~\cite{endert2012semantic}. These are natural building blocks. EDA adds structure that recommendation lacks, because a query selects a set and its result reveals why rows were available. It also adds a target, the destination, that is neither the next item nor a static preference.

\textbf{Benchmarks.}
IDEBench evaluates database performance under interactive exploration workloads~\cite{eichmannIDEBenchBenchmarkInteractive2020}. SIMBA simulates goal-driven sessions and measures goal completion~\cite{purichAdaptiveBenchmarkModeling2025}, so its goal queries could supply realistic destinations, although they are aggregates over dashboard views rather than sets of rows. Operational logs offer real usage but no goals. We characterised 462 SkyServer sessions~\cite{raddickTenYearsSkyserver2014} from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey~\cite{york_sloan_2000}, comprising 160k queries. Of these, 70\% are identifier lookups or return a single row, and 99.7\% instantiate one of 104 templates, with a median of two templates per session. Sessions do refine. In 12.5\% of transitions the analyst re-runs the same tables and predicates with new constants and gets a result no larger than before, and 40\% of sessions contain three such steps in a row. Only 0.02\% of transitions add a predicate, however. Exploration in this log is a parameter search within a fixed query form. Its destinations, if any, are entities rather than patterns, a kind our benchmark does not yet cover. None of these benchmarks varies the kind of destination, the ambiguity of the trajectory, or whether it switches, which is what Section~\ref{sec:first-step} adds.

\section{A First Step: Recovering Planted Destinations}
\label{sec:first-step}
%=============================================================================

We illustrate the principles with a destination benchmark, a simple scorer, and simple history baselines. The aim is to instantiate the benchmark, not to propose a final model.

\textbf{Benchmark.}
A seeded table of 12,000 shipments has categorical region, category, priority, transport mode, and sales channel attributes and a numeric price. We plant six destinations, two of each kind in Table~\ref{tab:kinds}, each in its own region$\times$category context. Within a context, urgency is raised (structural), a quarter of prices are inflated four-fold (distributional), or transport mode determines priority while leaving the priority marginal unchanged (compositional). The two destinations of a kind share a region, so early region-level steps fit both. Trajectories come in pairs, one towards each destination of a pair, so each direction has a competing destination; the \emph{distinguishing step} is the first at which the two differ. A further \emph{shared-context} pair plants a structural and a distributional destination in the same context, so only the step that filters for the pattern distinguishes them. In the terms of Figure~\ref{fig:example}, both analysts would narrow to healthcare shipments in Asia, after which one looks at urgent shipments and the other at expensive ones. Extensions are defined from observable values. A trajectory is eight selection queries, each capped at 200 rows. It moves from the region to the destination's context, to the pattern, and then to revisits that add incidental channel filters. \emph{Ambiguous} trajectories delay the distinguishing category step until step six. \emph{Detours} insert steps towards the competing destination, and \emph{switches} change destination after step four. \emph{Null} trajectories never reveal the distinguishing attribute. After every step, a copy of each model ranks a shared 100-row result. It contains 15 rows of each destination's extension, 10 in-context rows per destination that match neither, and 50 background rows. The copy is then discarded, so the shared result never enters the model's history. The paired directions see an identical result, which is the litmus test of Figure~\ref{fig:example}. By default these evaluation rows never appear in the trajectory, so success requires transferring evidence rather than recognising rows already seen. We report precision@10 ($\mathrm{prec@}k$ with $k=10$), for which random is .15. Earliness (Equation~\ref{eq:earliness}) is paired: a step counts only if, for both directions, precision reaches $\tau$ and exceeds precision on the competing destination. We use two thresholds. At the \emph{context level}, $\tau$ is twice random. A \emph{context oracle}, told the active destination's context and ranking its rows first, scores .6 (.3 for the shared pair) without recovering any pattern. At the \emph{pattern level}, $\tau$ is therefore set midway between this ceiling and 1. \emph{Lag} is earliness minus the distinguishing step.

\textbf{MDI.}
Multi-Dimensional Interestingness (MDI) instantiates Equation~\ref{eq:task} with one component for each kind of pattern destination in Table~\ref{tab:kinds}. It reads only results, never queries, so it is agnostic to the query language. For a tuple $t$ in the result $R$ being scored,
\begin{equation}
  s_q(t)=\alpha\,\rho\big(A(t)\big)+\beta\,\rho\big(D(t)\big)+\gamma\,\rho\big(N(t)\big),
  \label{eq:mdi}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ rank-normalises each component to $[0,1]$ over $R$, so that no component dominates through its scale. Evidence from the result observed at step $i$ is weighted by its age $q-i$, with $R$ itself treated as the most recent step.

The \emph{structural} component accumulates association rules mined from every observed result and from $R$:
\begin{equation}
  A(t)=\sum_{r} e^{-\lambda_r\,\mathrm{age}(r)}\,\frac{I(r)}{|r|}\,\mathbf{1}[t\models r],
  \label{eq:assoc}
\end{equation}
where $|r|$ is the number of items in rule $r$, and $I(r)$ combines its confidence, support, and lift~\cite{geng2006interestingness} with the magnitude of its leverage~\cite{piatetsky-shapiroDiscoveryAnalysisPresentation1991}. Rules that keep recurring as the session proceeds accumulate weight, while those not revisited fade.

The \emph{distributional} component rewards tuples whose values the session has concentrated on. For each attribute $a$, let $\tilde P_a$ be the session distribution of its values: the share of each value in each observed result, averaged with weights $e^{-\lambda_s\,\mathrm{age}}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  D(t)=\sum_{a} c_a\,\tilde P_a\big(t[a]\big),
  \label{eq:dist}
\end{equation}
where the concentration $c_a$ combines one minus Shannon evenness~\cite{shannon1948mathematical}, Simpson dominance~\cite{edwardsimpsonMeasurementDiversity1949}, the Gini coefficient~\cite{gini1921measurement}, Berger--Parker dominance~\cite{bergerDiversityPlanktonicForaminifera1970}, and one minus McIntosh diversity~\cite{mcintoshIndexDiversityRelation1967}, all computed on $\tilde P_a$. An attribute that a trajectory keeps narrowing, such as category for analyst A, becomes concentrated and so carries weight. An attribute it leaves untouched stays even and contributes little.

The \emph{novelty} component follows the inverse-frequency principle~\cite{sparckjones1972statistical}:
\begin{equation}
  N(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{1}{\log\big(2+f_a(t[a])\big)},
  \label{eq:novelty}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of attributes and $f_a(v)$ counts the occurrences of value $v$ across the session, so unseen values score highest. It promotes values the analyst has rarely seen, so that a ranking does not simply reinforce what has already been shown.

Numeric attributes are discretised into five equal-frequency bins. We use $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(.4,.4,.2)$, $\lambda_r=.05$, $\lambda_s=.1$, minimum support .08, and minimum confidence .1. We set these on development seeds and report ten held-out seeds.

\textbf{Comparisons.}
\emph{Naive MDI} sums the same three kinds of evidence on their raw scales, with diversity scored over pattern summaries and equal-width bins. Ablations remove history, novelty, or all but association rules (\emph{association only}). Two simple history models read only results, like MDI. \emph{Competing Models} adapts Monadjemi et al.~\cite{monadjemi2021competing}. Each hypothesis is a set of at most two attributes, under which a result's rows follow the decayed session distribution of their values on those attributes. Bayes' rule updates a posterior over the hypotheses after each step, and rows are ranked by the posterior predictive. The \emph{value profile} scores a row by the decayed session share of its values, which is $D$ without the concentration weights. Among history-free recommenders, \emph{frequency} ranks rows by how common their values are in the current result, \emph{similarity} by their mean cosine similarity to the other rows, and \emph{clustering} returns one representative per $k$-means cluster. \emph{Tuple recurrence} counts how often an entity was returned earlier, and \emph{random} samples uniformly. Two references are not ranked: the context oracle, and a query-aware \emph{predicate match} that scores rows by the decayed predicates the analyst used.

\textbf{What the benchmark shows.}
\emph{Recognising the context is easy; recovering the pattern is not.} MDI, Competing Models, and association only reach context-level earliness at the distinguishing step: step two on clear trajectories, step six on ambiguous ones. History-free methods give both analysts the same ranking, so they cannot beat the competing destination in both directions and never reach earliness, and no method does on null trajectories. The context oracle shows, however, that recognising the context alone suffices for this level. At the pattern level, MDI recovers structural destinations at the distinguishing step in every seed. It recovers distributional destinations in 80\% of seeds, with a mean lag of 0.6 steps, and compositional ones in 60\%, with a lag of 1.3. Shared-context destinations are the hardest. MDI ends at .75 against a context ceiling of .30, so it does find the pattern, but holds it in both directions in only 10\% of seeds.
\emph{Trajectory conditioning matters more than the scorer.} On clear trajectories, two simple history models match MDI (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Differences between MDI and Competing Models lie within the 95\% intervals ($|\Delta|\le.03$). They separate only in harder conditions, and in both directions. The best history-free recommender, frequency, reaches only .49. Tuple recurrence does no better than random, so recognising previously seen rows does not recover destinations. MDI's combination pays off mainly after switches on compositional destinations (.93, against .81 for Competing Models and .73 for association only). Removing novelty changes results by at most .05.
\emph{Plural interest matters, but only if evidence is combined carefully.} Naive MDI is blind to distributional destinations (.01 to .15, Table~\ref{tab:results}), because association scores dominate on raw scales and equal-width bins hide the heavy tail. Similarity, which favours typical rows, scores .01 there. Association only is best on clear compositional trajectories (.95 against .82) but adapt poorly after switches (Figure~\ref{fig:switch}). No fixed combination is best for every kind.
\emph{Persistence is the hard part of trajectory conditioning.} We tried adding a signal for values that a step narrows towards. It made results worse, especially after switches (structural .48), because it follows incidental filters as readily as persistent ones. Detours remain unsolved: on compositional detours every method scores between .52 and .62.
\emph{Queries help where only the pattern step distinguishes.} The query-aware reference recovers shared-context destinations (1.00 against .75) and distributional ones. It is worse on ambiguous trajectories (.71 against 1.00 for structural) because it follows every predicate equally.

\textbf{Limitations.}
The trajectories are scripted selection sequences and the destinations are planted. The benchmark tests capabilities; it does not show that these destinations are what real analysts pursue, or that surfacing them helps. The baselines are simple adaptations, not tuned systems. Every planted pattern is also a concentration on a few attributes, so the benchmark cannot yet show what modelling several kinds of interest adds over a single proximity model such as Competing Models. All code including the generator, models, per-session predictions, and complete results, including all conditions, budgets, and ablations, are available at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/query-data-predictor-DE86/README.md}. They are reproducible from seeds without a database.

\section{Research Agenda}
\label{sec:agenda}
%=============================================================================

\textbf{From destinations to interfaces.}
A predicted destination is only useful if the analyst can act on it. A destination can be rendered as highlighted supporting rows, the exceptions that break it, a shortcut query, a chart or even utilise previous explanation frameworks like FEDEX \cite{deutchFEDEXExplainabilityFramework2022}. The open questions are which rendering helps for which kind of destination, and when to intervene. Showing a destination too early risks steering the analyst; showing it too late saves nothing. Confidence over competing destinations, together with the earliness measure above, suggests intervention policies that can be compared in simulation by the steps they save before they are tested with people. Each rendering must also fit within interactive latency budgets as histories grow.

\textbf{Proposing destinations from the whole collection.}
Ranking a result helps only once the analyst has opened it. The larger opportunity is for the system to look ahead on the analyst's behalf. It would form hypotheses about the destination from the trajectory, evaluate each description $\varphi_d$ over the whole of $T$, and return ranked descriptions together with their extensions, including rows no query has yet returned. Analyst A would then see urgent technology shipments in Asia before asking for them, and could confirm or reject the hypothesis in a single step. This raises problems the database community is well placed to solve. The space of candidate descriptions grows combinatorially with attributes and values, so hypotheses must be generated and pruned within interactive latency, for example by reusing statistics maintained across the session, sampling, or pre-aggregation. Testing many hypotheses against the same data invites false discoveries, which must be controlled~\cite{zhao2017controlling}. A system that proposes only what it can find cheaply also risks steering analysts towards easy patterns rather than the ones they are pursuing. Exceptions are the natural bridge to discovery: a pattern sets an expectation, and the tuples that violate it are often what the analyst is after. Open-ended entity destinations, such as a new object in an astronomical survey, are the hardest case, and the SkyServer log suggests they are common in practice. On the evaluation side, SIMBA's goal-directed simulation~\cite{purichAdaptiveBenchmarkModeling2025} could provide realistic destinations, once its aggregate goal queries are mapped to the rows they summarise. Pairing these with planted destinations would combine realism with control.

\textbf{Persistence, change, and uncertainty.}
Our ablation shows that following every narrowing step can be misleading. Distinguishing a persistent direction from incidental filters, a switch from a detour, and a resumed thread from a new one requires models of concurrent investigation threads and change detection. These models should be evaluated on recovery after a change rather than on averages that hide it. When the evidence cannot separate destinations, as in our null trajectories, a system should report uncertainty rather than guess. This is the point at which brief explicit feedback becomes worth its cost. Since the best combination of evidence differs by kind, its weights should be inferred from context~\cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019} rather than fixed.

\textbf{Validation with analysts.}
Validation proceeds in two stages. First, collect ordinary exploration sessions from analysts carrying out profiling and discovery tasks. Have them, and independent assessors, judge how useful ranked rows were at recorded points, without revealing which method ranked them. Success means agreement beyond history-free and recurrence baselines, with calibrated uncertainty when evidence is ambiguous. Second, run controlled, counterbalanced comparisons of destination-aware assistance with unassisted exploration and an existing aid. Measure time to validated insight, independently assessed finding quality, diversity of exploration, and perceived interruption. A positive outcome reduces time and effort without reducing finding quality or diversity, and without increasing misleading conclusions. Accuracy alone is not enough if recommendations reinforce an early hypothesis or hide contradictory evidence. Longitudinal use should then test whether the benefits persist as analysts come to rely on the system.

\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================================

Systems that assist exploratory data analysis have learned to predict the next step and to explain the current one. We argue that the more useful question is where the analyst is heading. Answering it requires inferring interest implicitly, conditioning on the trajectory, recognising that interest takes several forms, and measuring progress with benchmarks that plant destinations and reward recovering them early. Our first benchmark shows that, on controlled trajectories, conditioning on the trajectory is what makes destinations recoverable. It also shows where current scorers fall short. The opportunity for the database community is to build systems that know where an exploration is going and help analysts get there sooner.
