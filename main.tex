\begin{abstract}

%Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) is central to modern data science, yet current EDA systems rely on explicit user feedback, example validation and active learning that disrupts cognitive flow and captures only a narrow view of analytical interest. We argue that implicit behavioral signals from natural exploration provide a richer, more authentic basis for recommendation. However, evaluating implicit recommenders poses its own challenge: realistic workloads produce high inter-query overlap that renders all methods statistically indistinguishable, masking differences in recommender capability. This paper makes four contributions: (1) a systematic critique of explicit feedback paradigms in EDA, showing their cognitive overhead and analytical limitations; (2) a workload recommender alignment theory that formally characterises when each class of recommender succeeds or fails, yielding a prescriptive recipe for constructing controlled benchmarks; (3) a Multi-Dimensional Interestingness (MDI) recommender that combines association rules, diversity measures, and novelty detection through temporal accumulation; and (4) a complementary evaluation methodology pairing realistic visualization-driven sessions (ecological validity) with a controlled adversarial benchmark (construct validity). On the adversarial benchmark, MDI achieves 36\% higher F1 than random selection, while on realistic drill-down workloads all methods perform similarly demonstrating that principled evaluation methodology is as important as recommender design.
Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is central to modern data science, yet most systems still rely on explicit user feedback, validation, or active learning. These mechanisms interrupt cognitive flow and capture only a limited view of analyst interest. We argue for two paradigm shifts in how database systems (DBMS) support EDA: (1) from explicit to implicit interest signals inferred from temporal patterns in natural exploration behaviour, and (2) from purely ``ecologically valid'' benchmarks to evaluation schemes that measure the ability to model and predict analytical interest over time. 


We develop a workload-recommender alignment framework that separates candidate suggestion from interest scoring and motivates complementary evaluation. An illustrative MDI scorer connects the proposed dimensions to concrete mechanisms. An audit of archived SIMBA-derived and SkyServer-derived workloads exposes candidate-availability constraints, while controlled diagnostics test history-conditioned pattern recovery. MDI recovers stipulated targets but adapts slowly to changed focus, and association-only scoring matches its performance in these tests. These findings motivate research on contextual evidence combination, changing interests, and benchmarks that distinguish behavioural prediction from useful analytical assistance.


\end{abstract}

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is an iterative process of examining data, formulating questions, and revising an investigation in response to what is found. It includes profiling data quality as well as discovering patterns; analysts' goals and working context affect which activities are useful~\cite{wongsuphasawat2019edagoals}. A query result provides candidate data for the next analytical step, but it does not tell a system which data matter to the analyst or which will become relevant as the investigation develops. A rare value may be a distraction. An ordinary-looking row may be central to a developing explanation.

Existing systems address parts of this problem. Active-learning approaches learn interests from explicit relevance feedback~\cite{dimitriadouAIDEActiveLearningBased2016}, while visualisation recommenders identify promising views using statistical and perceptual criteria~\cite{vartakSeedbEfficientDatadriven2015,wongsuphasawat2016voyager}. History-aware systems recommend queries or next actions~\cite{eirinakiQuerieCollaborativeDatabase2013,dezoysa2025exploract}, and session logs can inform the choice of interestingness measure~\cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019}. These advances motivate a further question: \emph{how can temporal patterns in ordinary exploration behaviour support implicit models of an analyst's evolving interests, enabling systems to anticipate relevant data several steps ahead?}

\textbf{A running example.}
Consider two analysts exploring shipments. Analyst A filters technology shipments, compares priorities across regions, and revisits urgent shipments from Asia. Analyst B filters healthcare shipments, compares prices across transport modes, and revisits premium shipments sent by air. Both then open the same broad result. Their histories suggest different interests even when the two patterns are equally common. Looking further ahead, a system might hypothesise that A will find exceptions to the region--priority relationship useful after intermediate comparisons, while B will investigate exceptions to the price--transport relationship. The desired prediction concerns the data or patterns that could matter later, not necessarily the exact sequence of queries each analyst will issue.

These are uncertain hypotheses. Narrowing a query for Asian shipments to urgent Asian shipments retains rows by set containment, whether or not the analyst noticed them. Inspecting an expensive delivery may be an attempt to reject an explanation rather than an expression of preference. An interest model must distinguish persistent investigative themes from repeated exposure, temporary detours, and genuine changes of focus.

We envision an \emph{interest-modelling capability} that maintains these hypotheses from queries, results, and available interaction traces. Its longer-term goal is to anticipate analytical relevance over multiple future steps, allowing downstream tools to prioritise inspection or surface useful evidence earlier. This differs from both predicting the next action and autonomously choosing an entire exploration sequence. We do not prescribe a particular interface. Faster insight discovery is the intended benefit, to be established through analyst studies rather than inferred from prediction accuracy alone.

The present work grounds this vision in a restricted setting: using accumulated result history to score tuples available in the current result. MDI illustrates temporal accumulation. An audit examines candidate availability for future membership, while controlled diagnostics test association recovery from different histories. These are complementary illustrations, not evidence that multi-step analytical relevance has already been predicted. Longer-horizon validation must compare predictions made from the same observed prefix, without revealing intervening actions, and assess relevance separately from recurrence.

Two connected research directions follow. The first is to infer evolving analytical interest without requiring relevance labels at every step, building on existing implicit-feedback and subjective-interest models. The second is to evaluate that inference by pairing observed or generated workloads with controlled stress tests. Workload overlap and candidate availability affect what can be predicted; neither high scores nor low scores alone establish whether a model captures interest.

Our contributions are a design space for implicit interest modelling (Section~\ref{sec:interest}), a workload--recommender alignment framework for designing diagnostic tests (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), and an illustrative MDI implementation and complementary evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:illustration}). These are starting points for the longer-horizon research programme, not a claim that anticipatory assistance or its human benefit has already been demonstrated.

\section{Related Work}

The database community has developed interactive exploration systems that enable users to navigate large datasets through visual interfaces, query-by-example paradigms and other novel interaction models. However many works that model a users interest over time rely on explicit feedback, example validation, or active learning. These mechanisms interrupt cognitive flow and capture only a limited view of analyst interest. Other systems that model interest implicitly are often not historically conditioned and do not evaluate whether the model anticipates future analytical relevance. 

\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Explicit feedback and active learning.}
AIDE uses active learning to acquire relevance labels on selected samples~\cite{dimitriadouAIDEActiveLearningBased2016}. DEXPLORER learns desired tuples and their ranking from labels and pairwise preferences~\cite{qinRankingDesiredTuples2021}. These approaches can model personal interests, but require analysts to supply judgements in addition to exploring the data. We argue that implicit signals reflect analysts actual behaviour \cite{kelly2003implicit}, can better encode temporal structure \cite{koren2009collaborative}, and preserve cognitive flow by avoiding context switching \cite{monsell2003task, rubinstein2001executive}.

\textbf{Implicit data and visualisation recommendation}

SeeDB ranks visualisations using deviation from a reference dataset~\cite{vartakSeedbEfficientDatadriven2015} implicitly in relation to a user query. Similarly, Voyager combines statistical and perceptual criteria with interactive browsing~\cite{wongsuphasawat2016voyager} however they both only utilise a singular notion of interest when deciding what to reccomend despite empriacle studies showing no single interestingness measure consistently predicts what analysts find interesting across domains and tasks~\cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019}. YMALDB \cite{drosouYmaldbExploringRelational2013} is similar in that it uses only frequent attribute sets to reccomend related tuples outside a query result.  FEDEX improves on this by identifies rows contributing to result-level diversity or exceptionality~\cite{deutchFEDEXExplainabilityFramework2022} with respect to many possible interestingness measures. Both FEDEX and YMALDB identify useful candidates without requiring a relevance label for each one. Their statistical or presentation criteria, however, do not by themselves establish which candidates will matter to a particular analyst later in a session. The gap is learning an evolving interpretation of those criteria from behaviour, rather than simply adding more measures.

\textbf{History-aware recommendation and longer-horizon interest}
Using exploration history to recommend a likely next action does not, by itself, establish a model of the analytical interests underlying that action. Query Steering proposed navigation informed by user interests and interaction histories~\cite{ugurcetintemelQuerySteeringInteractive2013}. QueRIE uses collaborative evidence from tuple or query-fragment profiles to recommend queries~\cite{eirinakiQuerieCollaborativeDatabase2013}, while ExplorAct models exploration histories to predict the next action type and column~\cite{dezoysa2025exploract}. These approaches provide valuable context-sensitive assistance. Nevertheless, an immediate action may be an intermediate comparison, a check of data quality, or an attempt to reject a hypothesis, rather than a direct expression of the analyst's underlying interest. The same action can serve several investigative goals, and the same goal can be pursued through different sequences of actions. Moreover, those goals may emerge or change as exploration proceeds.

Our vision is to maintain and update hypotheses about these possible directions, using temporal evidence to anticipate which data or patterns could become relevant several steps ahead. Next-action prediction can contribute to such a model, but accuracy on that task alone does not demonstrate that it captures long term analytical interest.

\textbf{Subjective-interest modelling and sequence generation}

There are several lines of work on subjective-interest modelling and sequence generation. Two broad approaches are using session logs to predict suitable interestingness measures \cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019} and inferring beliefs from BI interactions, schema structure, and other users' activity \cite{chanson2019belief}. The EDA interestingness survey further distinguishes several qualities of useful insights \cite{chansonInterestingnessMeasuresExploratory2025a}. However these works focus on explaining or scoring the current step, rather than anticipating later analytical relevance. ATENA generates exploration sessions with deep reinforcement learning \cite{barelAutomaticallyGeneratingData2020}, but generating a useful sequence from a dataset differs from forecasting a particular analyst's evolving interests from an observed session prefix. 


\textbf{Implicit recommendation and temporal modelling}
Prior work on implicit factorisation and ranking methods, temporal recommendation, and session-based recommendation demonstrates that useful signals can be extracted from ordinary behaviour~\cite{hu2008collaborative,rendle2009bpr,koren2009collaborative,hidasi2016session}. Follow The Clicks and Semantic Interaction show how analytical interactions can inform adaptive models~\cite{ottley2019clicks,endert2012semantic}. However, these works do not address the ambiguity of what those signals imply about future analytical relevance. ExpoMF and ReCRec explicitly model exposure~\cite{liang2016exposure,lin2024recrec}, and dynamic-missingness models distinguish changing intent from static preferences~\cite{wang2018modeling}. In EDA, query semantics reveal why a set was available, but not which rows were inspected or why. Extending such evidence across multiple future steps requires accounting for this ambiguity and reporting uncertainty.

\textbf{Benchmarks and evaluation.}

Evaluations of exploration assistance differ in both their evidence and their prediction targets. Human studies can assess usefulness and task performance, but an open underlying dataset alone does not provide a reusable benchmark: reproducing the evaluation also requires the tasks, interaction histories, and relevant judgements. Purpose-built tasks and synthetic targets permit controlled comparisons, although their conclusions depend on the particular preferences, patterns, and behaviours represented. Recorded-session benchmarks offer a complementary approach. For example, ExplorAct evaluates next-action recommendation using the REACT benchmark~\cite{dezoysa2025exploract}. Such reuse supports comparison across methods, but agreement with an observed next action does not by itself establish recovery of the analyst's underlying interests.

Operational SQL logs, such as SkyServer~\cite{raddickTenYearsSkyserver2014} from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) \cite{york_sloan_2000}, provide naturally occurring database interactions, but require careful selection and interpretation. In our experience SDSS is dominated by point lookup and templated queries many of which are not exploratory. The resulting sessions are often short, highly repetitive, and dominated by a few common queries. This makes them unsuitable for evaluating interest modelling, which requires longer sessions with more diverse investigative threads.

IDEBench and SIMBA provide more controlled, reusable foundations for evaluating database systems under exploratory workloads~\cite{eichmannIDEBenchBenchmarkInteractive2020,purichAdaptiveBenchmarkModeling2025}. But for the purposes of evaluating analytical interest, IDEBench make it difficult to specifiy the goal of the workload and whilst SIMBA does specify a goal, it does not provide a way to control the investigative threads that lead to that goal. 

We therefore advocate extending workload-based evaluation with controlled, history-conditioned pattern-recovery tests. A benchmark can embed known patterns and identify their associated tuples, then vary the preceding actions to provide evidence for different investigative targets while holding the current candidates fixed. Such tests assess whether a model recovers the stipulated target from session evidence rather than merely detecting globally conspicuous patterns. Our illustrative benchmark takes a step towards this evaluation programme. Its targets provide controlled ground truth for recovery, not proof of human interest.

\section{Analytical Interest as an Inference Problem}
\label{sec:interest}
\subsection{Scope and interface}
Let $R_q$ be the result available at exploration step $q$, and let
$\mathcal H_q=((Q_0,R_0),\ldots,(Q_{q-1},R_{q-1}))$ describe preceding queries and results in the session. We seek a scoring function
\begin{equation}
  s_q(t)=f(t,R_q,Q_q,\mathcal H_q),\qquad t\in R_q.
  \label{eq:interest}
\end{equation}
Its output is a relative ranking of candidate tuples. Scores need not be probabilities, and a system should be able to report insufficient evidence. The current result may contain data not previously encountered, but searching beyond that result is outside our scope. Optional inspection traces can enrich $\mathcal H_q$ when an interface records them. The illustrative implementation below uses result histories only, so it cannot distinguish all behaviours expressible by this interface.

We use \emph{analytical interest} to mean the potential value of data to the analyst's evolving investigation. It is a latent, contextual construct. This differs from \emph{statistical interestingness}, which assigns value according to a specified property such as deviation or association strength. It also differs from \emph{exposure}: a row's presence in a result does not show that the analyst inspected it. A successful model must connect statistical evidence to a session while acknowledging that this connection can be wrong.

The initial setting is a single analyst exploring one tabular dataset through filters, comparisons, and revisits. The minimum input regime requires no reusable corpus of other users' sessions. Such a corpus, when available, can supply learned priors or collaborative evidence; a single active analyst does not preclude a pretrained model. Query results must provide stable entity identities if tuple recurrence is measured. Aggregation, joins that change entity granularity, and incomplete logs require additional semantics. The controlled sessions in Section~\ref{sec:illustration} are stipulated sequences, not estimates of natural session length.

\subsection{Four Dimensions of Analytical Interest}
\label{sec:interest:dimensions}
Drawing on Hilderman and Hamilton's treatment of interestingness measures~\cite{robertj.hildermanKnowledgeDiscoveryMeasures2001}, we organise evidence for analytical interest along four dimensions: structural, distributional, temporal, and compositional. These dimensions define a design space for implicit-interest models, rather than a fixed taxonomy of analyst goals. They complement the qualities of interestingness identified by Chanson et al., including peculiarity, novelty, relevance, surprise, diversity, and presentation~\cite{chansonInterestingnessMeasuresExploratory2025a}: temporal evidence, for example, can inform both relevance and novelty.

\noindent \textbf{Structural interest.}
This dimension captures relationships between variables, including correlations, associations, and dependency structures. Association-rule measures such as support, confidence, lift, and the J-measure~\cite{goodman1991rule} provide candidate evidence about these relationships. Repeated investigation of a relationship may suggest that it matters to the analyst, but its statistical strength alone does not establish its relevance to the investigation. MDI's association component $A(t,q)$ illustrates how rule evidence can be accumulated over a session.

\noindent \textbf{Distributional interest.}
This dimension captures anomalies, uneven distributions, and contrasts in the diversity of observed values. Candidate measures include Shannon entropy~\cite{shannon1948mathematical}, Simpson concentration, the Gini coefficient~\cite{gini1921measurement}, Berger--Parker dominance~\cite{bergerDiversityPlanktonicForaminifera1970}, and McIntosh diversity~\cite{mcintoshIndexDiversityRelation1967}. These measures describe different distributional properties; none alone determines what an analyst will find useful. MDI's diversity component $D(t,q)$ combines distributional evidence from the session, while a broader model could learn which properties matter to the current investigation.

\noindent \textbf{Temporal interest.}
Analytical goals may persist, evolve, or be abandoned over the course of a session. Frequent-episode mining~\cite{mannila1997discovery} offers a basis for representing recurring event patterns, motivating attention to the order and proximity of analytical actions. For interest inference, the challenge is to distinguish a persistent investigation thread from repeated exposure caused by query structure. MDI provides a limited instantiation through temporally weighted association evidence: recently reinforced patterns retain influence, while evidence not revisited fades.

\noindent \textbf{Compositional interest.}
Analysts may investigate natural groupings or contrasts between sub-populations defined by combinations of attributes. A combination can matter even when its individual values are common, so compositional evidence requires attention to joint structure rather than rarity alone. The inverse-frequency principle~\cite{sparckjones1972statistical} motivates MDI's novelty component $N(t,q)$, which rewards under-represented attribute values. This is only a partial treatment of compositional interest; modelling meaningful combinations and contrasts requires richer evidence.

These four dimensions need not be instantiated exactly as in MDI, nor are they independent: a structural relationship may become relevant because it persists over time or distinguishes a particular sub-population. Table~\ref{tab:dimensions} summarises possible cues and competing explanations. The objective is to infer their significance from the evolving session, not to equate a high statistical score with analytical interest.

Information-foraging theory offers a useful motivation: an analyst's successive choices may provide evidence of perceived value~\cite{pirolli2005information}. It does not entail that every returned tuple is interesting. A query is a choice of an operation under constraints, and its output contains both sought and incidental data. In the running example, repeatedly investigating technology shipments may strengthen an association hypothesis, while recurring background shipments should not automatically receive the same interpretation.

This distinction suggests a system architecture with three responsibilities (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). An observation layer records what the analyst could encounter and what operations produced it. A model maintains competing explanations of the session. A scoring layer applies that evidence to current candidates, ideally exposing its basis and uncertainty. Keeping these responsibilities explicit makes it possible to change an interface or a ranking model without changing the meaning of the evidence being collected.

Preserving analytical flow motivates inferring from ordinary exploration rather than repeatedly eliciting relevance judgements. Whether this actually reduces burden is an empirical question. An inaccurate, distracting, or overconfident recommendation can impose its own cost. Explicit feedback also remains valuable when ambiguity cannot be resolved from observed behaviour.

\section{An Illustrative Model and Evaluation}
\label{sec:mdi}
\label{sec:illustration}
\label{sec:experiments}

We illustrate the separation between candidate suggestion and interest scoring with the simple policy $C_q=R_q$ and the Multi-Dimensional Interestingness (MDI) scorer. This implementation does not attempt database-wide candidate discovery. Instead, it lets us examine how accumulated session evidence influences ranking. We pair an audit of two archived exploration workloads with controlled tests of history-conditioned pattern recovery. These provide complementary evidence, not a single leaderboard or a validation of the full vision.

\subsection{MDI as an Illustrative Instantiation}
MDI combines three scores for a candidate tuple $t$:
\begin{equation}
 s_{\mathrm{MDI}}(t,q)=\alpha A(t,q)+\beta D(t,q)+\gamma N(t,q).
 \label{eq:mdi-score}
\end{equation}
The four dimensions from Section~\ref{sec:interest:dimensions} map to this implementation as follows.

\noindent\textbf{Structural evidence.}
$A$ accumulates contributions from matching association rules mined in individual query results and retained across the session. Rule contributions combine confidence, support, lift, and absolute leverage, with rule-length normalisation. The implementation uses leverage rather than computing the J-measure discussed as a candidate measure in the design space.

\noindent\textbf{Distributional evidence.}
$D$ scores tuples using matching summaries. Their contributions are weighted by diversity statistics computed over summary counts, combining Shannon, Simpson, Gini, Berger--Parker, and McIntosh measures. This is a summary-based scoring heuristic, not the marginal increase in diversity obtained by adding a tuple.

\noindent\textbf{Temporal evidence.}
Rule and summary contributions decay exponentially with age. Repeatedly supported patterns can accumulate evidence while older contributions lose influence. This implements recency weighting, not an explicit model of competing goals or an identified change of analytical focus.

\noindent\textbf{Compositional evidence.}
$N$ averages inverse log-frequency scores for individual attribute values over observed results. It can promote under-represented values but does not fully capture the novelty of joint attribute combinations. MDI therefore instantiates only part of the compositional dimension.

We retain illustrative weights $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(.5,.3,.2)$, minimum support $.08$, confidence threshold $.1$, rule decay $.05$, and summary decay $.1$. These are inherited settings, not optimised on the diagnostics. MDI incorporates the current result before scoring, so the comparisons test what accumulated evidence adds beyond current-result structure. Component scales are not calibrated, and neither exposure nor uncertainty is explicitly modelled. The purpose is to expose design questions, including when combining evidence fails to improve ranking.

\subsection{Workload Opportunities and Limitations}
\label{sec:workload-audit}

\textbf{Two sources of exploration workloads.}
The archived SIMBA-derived sessions originate from generated exploration, while the SkyServer-derived sessions originate from recorded astronomical database usage~\cite{purichAdaptiveBenchmarkModeling2025,raddickTenYearsSkyserver2014}. These sources offer different kinds of context for evaluation. We audit the available gap-$1$ result snapshots to ask a narrower question: how much opportunity do they provide to predict future membership when candidates are restricted to the current result?

The audit includes one SIMBA-derived session with 14 transitions and seven SkyServer-derived sessions with 608 transitions. These are the available snapshots, not the larger session counts reported in the original experimental draft. Repeated records from different recommenders are deduplicated by session and query pair, checking that their snapshots agree. We compare sets of exact full rows, including column names and aggregate values. Because the SIMBA archive includes grouped results, this is a representation-level audit, not a measurement of stable underlying entity recurrence.

\textbf{What the comparison establishes.}
For current-result candidates, retention is the fraction also present in the next result; coverage is the fraction of that next result available among the candidates. They give the random-precision reference and unbudgeted recall ceiling, respectively (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). All audited results are nonempty. The SkyServer samples frequently provide no matching candidates, while the SIMBA sample offers greater average coverage but still has six zero-overlap transitions. Neither uniform containment nor universal failure of realistic workloads follows. Changes in projection or aggregates can also break row matches. The comparison motivates checking candidate eligibility and representation before attributing ranking scores to interest inference.

The legacy model comparisons used a budget based on the future result size, unlike the fixed-budget diagnostics below. We therefore do not combine their scores. A common-budget evaluation across prediction gaps, and comparison of current-result against seen-so-far candidate policies, remain necessary to test the multi-step behavioural prediction objective in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. The controlled tests instead isolate whether session evidence affects recovery of known patterns.

\subsection{Controlled Diagnostic Protocol}

\textbf{The basic experiment.}
Consider two sessions that finish at exactly the same candidate result. One history repeatedly exposes pattern A; the other exposes pattern B. Can a scorer change its ranking to favour the pattern supported by the respective history? By using different entities in the histories and candidate result, we test transfer of pattern evidence rather than recognition of previously returned rows.

We use five categorical shipment attributes: region, category, price band, priority, and transport. Pattern A combines Asia, technology, high price, and urgent priority; pattern B combines Europe, healthcare, premium price, and air transport. Each evaluation result contains 60 distinct entities: 15 satisfy A, 15 satisfy B, and 30 satisfy neither. The generator stipulates the active target; target labels and entity IDs are excluded from model features. Distinct entities may have identical attribute values, but evaluation uses their stable IDs. The primary task selects ten candidates and measures the fraction satisfying the active target. Uniform random selection has expected precision $.25$.

\textbf{Four questions about session evidence.}
All conditions use the paired, identical candidate result, with the following histories:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em,topsep=2pt,itemsep=2pt,parsep=0pt]
 \item \textbf{History: does previous evidence transfer?} Four preceding results each contain 24 active-pattern and 24 background rows, with entities distinct from the candidate result. Paired sessions differ in which pattern their histories support.
 \item \textbf{Persistence: can repeated distractors mislead?} Each history result instead contains 24 active-pattern rows, four fresh background rows, and 20 fixed background entities also present among the candidates. Recurrence favours these distractors; pattern evidence favours the target.
 \item \textbf{Switch: can the model change direction?} Four history results support one pattern, followed by four supporting the other. We probe the shared candidate result before the switch and after each new history result. The probes themselves enter observed history; this tests adaptation to new evidence, not prediction of a switch before it occurs.
 \item \textbf{Null: does the history identify a target?} Each of four history results contains 12 rows from each pattern and 24 background rows. Both paired sessions receive identical observations but different evaluator-assigned targets. Recovering either pattern is possible; identifying which target was assigned is not.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Comparators and measurements.}
We compare MDI with association-only scoring, MDI reset before every result, direct entity-recurrence counts, and the repository's frequency, similarity, clustering, sampling, and random baselines. The first two isolate the contribution of score combination and accumulated history. These are illustrative comparators, not an exhaustive state-of-the-art evaluation. Frequency uses current-result frequencies; the sampling implementation's numeric-variance scores tie on these categorical results, making its ranking depend on candidate order.

We use ten independent seeds, both target directions, and budgets $k\in\{5,10,25\}$, with precision@10 primary. Missing recommendations count as misses against the fixed budget. Alternative budgets receive copies of the same pre-query state, and no model sees future results. Results are averaged over directions within each seed; paired differences and descriptive 95\% $t$ intervals use these ten seed-level values, not individual probes as independent replicates. We also report \emph{target contrast}: the selected fraction satisfying the active pattern minus the fraction satisfying its alternative. This separates directional selectivity from detection of both patterns. Rule-decay settings $0$ and $.2$ supplement the default $.05$ without selecting a best setting on the evaluation seeds.

\section{Challenges and Future Directions}
\label{sec:agenda}
% \vspace{-0.4em}
The illustrative results motivate four connected research directions, moving from interpreting session evidence to establishing whether acting on it helps analysts.
%\textbf{Real-time interest model adaptation.}
%EDA presents a fundamentally different challenge from traditional recommendation: user interests can shift dramatically within a single session as new patterns are discovered. Developing online learning algorithms that adapt rapidly while maintaining computational efficiency calls for advances in incremental pattern mining, streaming summarisation, and principled strategies for balancing exploration and exploitation.

%\textbf{Richer implicit signals, interpretability, and trust.}
%Unlike entertainment recommendation, EDA recommendations directly influence analytical conclusions. Future systems will need to integrate richer implicit signals from visual interfaces (e.g., clicks, hovers, dwell times) with structural signals from query logs, while providing transparent explanations of why specific tuples or regions are recommended. This requires domain-specific explanation frameworks that expose how recommendations may bias exploration, going beyond generic explainable AI techniques.

%\textbf{Cross-domain interest transfer.}
%Analysts often work across multiple datasets, domains, and projects. Understanding how exploration patterns transfer across data contexts could reduce cold-start effects and improve recommendation quality, motivating transfer learning and meta-learning approaches tailored to analytical interests rather than consumer preferences.

%\textbf{Extending the alignment framework.}
%The workload recommender alignment framework in Section~\ref{sec:theory} currently covers a small set of classical recommenders. Extending it to characterise neural and language model-based recommenders, derive their failure conditions, and prescribe diagnostic benchmarks is an important open problem. Within this framework, adaptive schemes that learn to weight different dimensions of interestingness (e.g., MDI’s $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$) based on workload characteristics are a natural next step.

%\textbf{User studies and longitudinal evaluation.}
%Finally, longitudinal user studies are needed to assess whether implicit recommendation systems enhance or constrain analytical discovery compared to explicit-feedback baselines. Such studies should examine not only task performance, but also trust, sense-making, and the diversity of insights uncovered over extended exploration sessions.

\textbf{Disambiguating analytical goals.}
The same query sequence can support several investigative goals. Query semantics and optional inspection traces (e.g., clicks or dwell times) may help distinguish deliberate investigation from incidental exposure, building on exposure-aware recommendation~\cite{liang2016exposure,lin2024recrec}. Rather than committing prematurely to one interpretation, a model could maintain competing hypotheses, express uncertainty, or request occasional clarification. Progress requires better agreement with independent analyst judgements than current-result and recurrence baselines, while recognising when the available evidence cannot identify a goal.

\textbf{Detecting change and combining evidence.}
The Switch diagnostic exposes the cost of retaining stale evidence. Change detection and separate memories for concurrent investigation threads could distinguish a new goal from a temporary detour. Context-dependent weighting~\cite{somechPredictingWhatInteresting2019} could also determine when association, diversity, or novelty evidence is useful, rather than assuming a fixed combination. Evaluation should measure adaptation after a change and recovery of resumed threads, not only session-average accuracy. Tests across domains and component ablations should establish whether richer models generalise beyond the patterns used to design them.

\textbf{Choosing an interaction paradigm.}
Interest scores could support highlighted tuples, contrasting groups, suggested queries, or visualisations. Which form of assistance is useful, and when should the system intervene? Candidate suggestion and ranking must operate within interactive latency and memory budgets, while explanations should expose the historical evidence and uncertainty behind a recommendation. Joins and aggregation further require clarity about what a candidate represents. Comparing presentation choices should assess both analytical usefulness and interruption, while retaining analyst control over the investigation.

\textbf{Validating analytical benefit.}
User studies should first test whether predictions agree with analysts' assessments of usefulness, then whether presenting those predictions improves analysis. Controlled comparisons with unassisted exploration and an appropriate existing aid should measure time to validated insights, finding quality, exploration diversity, and perceived burden. Counterbalanced tasks and independent assessment of findings can reduce evaluation bias. Higher prediction accuracy is insufficient if recommendations reinforce an early hypothesis or hide contradictory evidence; longitudinal studies should examine whether benefits and appropriate trust persist with use.

\section{Conclusion}
Exploration histories offer evidence about what data may matter to an analyst, but that evidence is ambiguous. We propose session-conditioned scoring of current-result tuples as a reusable capability, with statistical properties, exposure, and latent interest kept distinct. Controlled tests can reveal whether a model follows stipulated session evidence and where it fails. Human validation must establish whether those distinctions correspond to useful analytical judgements. The opportunity is to build data systems that can act on evolving interest while making the basis and limits of their inferences explicit.
